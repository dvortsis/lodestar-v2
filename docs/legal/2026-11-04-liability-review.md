# Operator liability review: US federal and state law (2026-11-04)

**Purpose.** The owner asked whether Lodestar's maker can be held liable for what its users do, and what language the software should carry to make clear that it's for lawful use only, that the user understands the risks and that the user accepts responsibility. This memo records the review and the reasoning behind `docs/legal/terms-of-use.md` and `docs/decisions/2026-11-04-legal-terms.md`.

**This is research by an AI assistant, not legal advice,** and no lawyer has reviewed it. Case holdings below were checked against secondary sources (law-firm alerts, case summaries, the statutes' own text) in November 2026; pin cites and recent decisions should be confirmed against the opinions before anyone relies on them. **Recommendation: have a US attorney who handles copyright and online terms review the Terms of Use before the first public release (v0.5.1).** It's a small document, and that review is cheap compared with the exposure it addresses.

## 1. What a disclaimer can and cannot do
A terms-of-use text does three real things:
1. **It allocates responsibility between the maker and the operator.** The operator agrees that running an instance, enabling sources and acting on results are their decisions, and agrees to indemnify the maker for claims that arise from them. This is a contract, enforceable against the operator who accepted it.
2. **It disclaims warranties and limits liability** for claims *by the operator* (the software failed, a filter missed something, the index was wrong). Together with AGPL-3.0 §§15 and 16 this is the strongest, most settled protection the project has.
3. **It is evidence of intent.** A court weighing whether the maker *induced* infringement looks at what the maker said and designed. A text that says "lawful use only", refuses infringing uses and backs this with real filtering is evidence against inducement.

What it **cannot** do:
- **It cannot bind third parties.** A rights holder who sues the maker never agreed to the terms. Against them the maker's defence rests on the law in §2, not on the text.
- **It cannot cure inducement.** isoHunt had disclaimers and said it hosted no content. The Ninth Circuit held its operator liable anyway, because his statements and design showed an object of promoting infringement (*Columbia Pictures v. Fung*, 710 F.3d 1020 (9th Cir. 2013); settled for $110 million). What the maker *does* and *says in marketing* matters more than any disclaimer.
- **It cannot exempt the maker from its own fraud, wilful injury or violation of law** in most states (for example California Civil Code §1668), so the text must carve those out or risk the clause, or the whole terms, being struck.

So the honest answer to "will this mean I won't be held liable" is: **no text guarantees that.** The text plus the design decisions in §4 put the project in the position US law protects, which is a general-purpose tool with substantial lawful uses, not marketed for infringement, not operated by its maker.

## 2. The theories a plaintiff would use against the maker

### 2.1 Secondary copyright liability (the main exposure)
- **Direct infringement** needs the defendant to copy, distribute or display the work. Lodestar's maker does none of that. Lodestar itself, by default, fetches only the info-dictionary. An operator who enables the micro-piece prober (architecture §6) downloads fragments of content, which is the operator's act, not the maker's.
- **Contributory infringement** needs knowledge of specific infringement plus material contribution (*A&M Records v. Napster*, 239 F.3d 1004 (9th Cir. 2001); *Perfect 10 v. Amazon*, 508 F.3d 1146 (9th Cir. 2007) on search engines). A maker who distributes software and never sees any instance's index has no knowledge of specific infringements on that instance.
- **Inducement** (*MGM v. Grokster*, 545 U.S. 913 (2005)) needs distribution "with the object of promoting its use to infringe", shown by clear expression or other affirmative steps. *Grokster* named three signals: targeting a known infringing audience, failing to develop filtering tools, and a business model that depends on infringement volume. *Fung* applied this to a torrent search site and added that red-flag knowledge from the operator's own inducing conduct also forfeits the DMCA safe harbours. *Arista v. Lime Group*, 784 F. Supp. 2d 398 (S.D.N.Y. 2010) applied it to a software maker (LimeWire) for its marketing and its refusal to filter.
- **The *Sony* defence** (*Sony v. Universal*, 464 U.S. 417 (1984)): distributing a product capable of substantial non-infringing uses isn't contributory infringement by itself. *Grokster* kept this and limited it only where inducement is shown.

**Where Lodestar stands.** Metadata-only by default; a mandatory child-safety filter and switchable content filters (the opposite of *Grokster*'s "failed to develop filtering tools"); no advertising or revenue tied to volume; no profiles of people; private-source isolation; politeness caps. The exposures that remain are **(a)** bundling plugins for Anna's Archive and LibGen, both of which are now under US default judgments and broad injunctions (LibGen: $30 million, S.D.N.Y. 2024; Anna's Archive: $19.5 million, S.D.N.Y. May 2026, plus an Ohio injunction in *OCLC v. Anna's Archive*), **(b)** tracker-announce peer finding and the micro-piece prober, which make an instance a swarm participant, and **(c)** any marketing, screenshot, release note or fixture that shows Lodestar being used to find commercial films, music or software. (a) is the single largest inducement signal a plaintiff could point to, independent of any disclaimer; it is flagged to the owner in the decisions file. (b) is already off by default behind a consent screen, which the Terms reinforce. (c) is a standing rule in the decisions file.

### 2.2 The DMCA safe harbours (17 U.S.C. §512)
- §512(d) protects "information location tools" (search engines, indexes) and §512(a) transitory communications, **but only service providers**, and only with a designated agent registered with the Copyright Office ($6 per filing, renewed every three years, online only) and a repeat-infringer policy.
- The maker of self-hosted software isn't a service provider for instances other people run, so the safe harbours neither apply nor are needed for them. **Each operator** who exposes an instance to others is the service provider for it, and the Terms say so (§10).
- **Recommendation:** if the owner ever runs a public demo instance, a hosted index, or a site that accepts user content, register a DMCA agent for it first and publish a takedown address. The GitHub repository is covered by GitHub's own DMCA process.

### 2.3 Section 230 (47 U.S.C. §230)
- §230(c)(1) shields providers of interactive computer services from liability for third-party content, **but §230(e)(2) excludes intellectual-property claims**, so it never helped against copyright claims. It might cover defamation-type claims about indexed titles, for an operator.
- A software maker who doesn't run the service isn't the "provider" for that instance anyway. **Status in 2026:** the Sunset Section 230 Act (S.3546, passed the Senate Judiciary Committee; H.R. 10332 in the House) would repeal §230 two years after enactment. Nothing in Lodestar's position depends on §230, so the project doesn't need to track it.

### 2.4 Criminal law
Criminal copyright (17 U.S.C. §506; 18 U.S.C. §2319) needs wilful infringement for commercial advantage or above value thresholds. The KickassTorrents indictment (N.D. Ill. 2016) rested on advertising revenue and the operator's control of the site. A non-commercial, metadata-only tool whose maker runs no site is far from that pattern. The child-exploitation statutes (18 U.S.C. §§2252, 2252A) are the reason the CSAM blocklist is non-negotiable; the reporting duty in 18 U.S.C. §2258A applies to providers of electronic communication or remote computing services, which a self-hosting operator generally isn't, but an operator who serves a public instance should take advice.

### 2.5 Other claims an operator, not the maker, would face
Computer-misuse and anti-circumvention claims from sites that forbid scraping (the project's Q2 rule: a plugin ships only where automated access is allowed, and a grey case goes to the owner); ISP terms; GDPR for IP addresses (architecture §18.7 already treats them as personal data). The Terms put each of these on the operator (§4).

## 3. Making the terms enforceable: contract-formation law
US courts enforce online terms when the user had **reasonably conspicuous notice** and took an **unambiguous act of assent**. The Ninth Circuit applies this strictly: *Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble*, 763 F.3d 1171 (2014) (browsewrap fails); *Berman v. Freedom Financial*, 30 F.4th 849 (2022) (the user must be told that the click means agreement); *Chabolla v. ClassPass* (9th Cir. Feb. 2025) and *Godun v. JustAnswer* (9th Cir. 2025) (faint notice far from the button fails). The Second Circuit (*Specht v. Netscape*, 306 F.3d 17 (2002); *Meyer v. Uber*, 868 F.3d 66 (2017)) reaches the same two-part test with less formalism. New York's highest court followed suit in *Wu v. Uber* (2024). E-SIGN and the state UETAs make the electronic record valid.

**What this means for the app** (adopted in the decisions file):
- a **required checkbox**, unticked by default, on the first-run password screen, whose label says that ticking it means accepting the Terms, with the Terms one click away and readable in full inside the app;
- the Terms **served from the binary** (no internet needed), with the version shown;
- the acceptance **recorded with the Terms version**, and shown again when the version changes in substance;
- the same notice in the Unraid template description and the README, so it's seen before install too (browsewrap alone wouldn't bind anyone, but it adds notice).

The acceptance binds the person who set up the instance. For other users of that instance, the Terms make the operator responsible for them (§4), which is the usual pattern.

## 4. Warranty disclaimers and liability limits: state-law variations
- **UCC §2-316** requires a disclaimer of the implied warranty of merchantability to mention merchantability and, if written, to be **conspicuous**; "as is" also works if conspicuous (UCC §1-201(b)(10): capitals, contrasting type, a heading). The Terms use capitals and headings for §§7 and 8. Whether Article 2 applies to a free software licence at all is contested; the conspicuous form costs nothing and covers the case where it does.
- **UCC §2-719(3)**: limits on consequential damages fail if unconscionable; for personal injury in consumer goods they're presumed unconscionable. Not Lodestar's situation, but the carve-out language covers it.
- **States that forbid implied-warranty disclaimers in consumer sales** (Maryland Com. Law §2-316.1 is explicit; Massachusetts G.L. c.106 §2-316A and several others are reported to do the same; Magnuson-Moss restricts "as is" where a written warranty is given). Lodestar is free and gives no written warranty, so most of these don't bite, but §13 of the Terms yields to them anyway rather than fight.
- **California Civil Code §1668**: no contract may exempt a party from its own fraud, wilful injury to person or property, or violation of law. In 2025 the California Supreme Court held that §1668 also voids *limits* (not just exemptions) on damages for wilful injury (*New England Country Foods v. VanLaw Food Products*; confirm the cite). The Terms carve these out expressly (§§8, 13).
- **New Jersey's TCCWNA** (N.J.S.A. 56:12-14 to -18): a consumer contract may not say that some provisions "may be void or unenforceable in some jurisdictions" **without stating which ones are void in New Jersey**. A plain "void where prohibited" line invites a class action; a clause beginning "to the fullest extent permitted by law" has been held acceptable, and *Spade v. Select Comfort*, 232 N.J. 504 (2018), requires actual harm. The Terms (§13) name the New Jersey position expressly and use "to the fullest extent permitted by applicable law" throughout.
- **Indemnities** by consumers are enforceable in most states but read narrowly; the Terms limit the indemnity to the operator's own conduct, sources they enable and breaches of the terms.
- **Governing law and venue** clauses are generally enforced, but many states refuse to let a choice-of-law clause strip their consumers of mandatory protections. The Terms choose Colorado (the owner's state, 2026-11-04) and yield to mandatory local law.
- **Colorado.** Colorado follows the uniform UCC text (C.R.S. §4-2-316: name merchantability, be conspicuous) and has no statute barring implied-warranty disclaimers in consumer sales that this review found. Exculpatory clauses are "closely scrutinised" under the four *Jones v. Dressel* factors, 623 P.2d 370 (Colo. 1981): a duty to the public, the nature of the service, whether the contract was fairly entered into, and whether the intent is clear and unambiguous; failing any one defeats the clause (*Brigance v. Vail Summit Resorts*, 883 F.3d 1243 (10th Cir. 2018)), and clarity is the usual battleground (*Shive v. 24 Hour Fitness*, Colo. App. 2025). Free, optional software owes no duty to the public, the checkbox makes the contract fairly entered, and the plain-language sections plus the conspicuous §§7–8 are what the fourth factor asks for. The Colorado Consumer Protection Act (C.R.S. §6-1-101 et seq.) reaches deceptive practices, which no disclaimer cures, so the Terms and all project material must describe the software accurately.
- **No address is required.** Nothing in US or Colorado law requires a private individual who publishes free software to print a postal address in its terms. The Terms give the public repository as the contact, which is enough for complaints to reach the project. A postal address becomes necessary only for a DMCA designated-agent registration (which needs the service provider's physical street address and the agent's mail address, and only matters if a hosted service ever exists), and for commercial email under CAN-SPAM, which the project doesn't send. Keeping a home address out of a public repository is the safer choice for a private individual.
- **Age.** The Terms require operators to be 18, which also keeps the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act out of scope and matches the adult-content handling.

## 5. Recommendations (what the decisions file adopts, and what needs the owner)
Adopted:
1. Ship `docs/legal/terms-of-use.md` in the binary and show it with a required, version-bound acceptance checkbox on the first-run password screen; show it again on a substantive version change.
2. Add a **Legal** section to Settings → About (Terms, AGPL, third-party licences).
3. Link the Terms from the README, the Unraid template description and the user guide's chapter 1; keep a one-line "lawful use only" sentence in each.
4. Keep the Restricted and Overlay consent screens, and tighten their closing lines so the operator confirms lawfulness and responsibility, and link the Terms from them.
5. A standing content rule: nothing the project publishes (README, screenshots, release notes, fixtures, plugin descriptions) shows or suggests finding commercial films, music, software or books without permission. Fixture titles stay generic or public-domain.

Needs the owner:
6. **Fill in the governing-law state and the contact address** in Terms §14.
7. **Have an attorney review the Terms before v0.5.1.**
8. **Decide whether plugins for Anna's Archive and LibGen ship at all**, given the 2024 and 2026 US judgments and injunctions against those sites. Options: drop them; ship them unlisted (a user supplies the manifest); or keep them as Restricted with the owner accepting the inducement exposure. This review's recommendation is the first or second option. This is a scope decision, so it isn't taken here.
9. If a public demo, hosted index or user-content site ever exists, register a DMCA agent for it first.
